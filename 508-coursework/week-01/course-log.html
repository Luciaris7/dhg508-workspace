About this course

We will use AI to address real historical and humanities research needs. Each week, you will build something small, examine what it does, and explain the judgments behind it. The course will develop in response to your interests and the problems you encounter.

Why this course?

Use AI to address real research needs and develop the ability to do work that matters to you.

Why now?

AI is becoming part of research work. Learning to use it is part of maintaining our ability to do that work well. Bo treats the advances since November 2025 as a turning point in his own practical work; we will examine what current systems can actually do with concrete examples.

Two dated milestones are Google's Gemini 3 announcement, 18 November 2025 and Anthropic's Claude Opus 4.5 announcement, 24 November 2025. These are developers' reports about their systems, not a substitute for testing our research tasks.

Software engineers are learning new ways to work with AI, too. Stanford's CS146S: The Modern Software Developer combines practical work with coding agents and attention to their limitations. Our course addresses historical research needs while developing related skills:

  • Workflow: a sequence of steps connecting a research question, sources, and a result.
  • Agentic work: giving an AI agent a task it can pursue using tools, with appropriate checks.
  • Orchestration: coordinating tasks, tools, and checks across a larger workflow.

Why informed judgment?

Using AI—and predicting what AI can do—requires informed judgment. We will practise that judgment through weekly projects and workshops.

About Bo An

Bo is a historian of computing, media, and modern China. His Yale PhD examined the history of computing in China, 1949–2000. His research experience includes Harvard's Fairbank Center and Microsoft Research's FATE group. He also builds research software; this course connects historical inquiry with that practical work.

How the course works

  • One mini-project each week: usually using AI to make a small program or do a useful research task.
  • Show and discuss next class: bring your result, explain your process, and respond to questions.
  • Build progressively: begin with sample data, then work with your own sources.
  • Accumulate a workflow: use the weekly work to develop skills and components toward your final project and capstone. Every task need not directly become part of the final project.
  • A workshop in every class: everyone must engage. An attempt, a careful test, a useful question, or helping diagnose a problem can make your participation visible.

Informed critique in every workshop

Critique should be grounded in experience and evidence. In weekly projects and workshops, reflect on your work:

  1. What did you try, and what actually happened?
  2. What questions did you ask? How did you respond to questions about your work?
  3. Where did the material, tool, or answer fail?
  4. How did you mitigate the problem—reduce its likelihood or impact—and check again?
  5. What remains uncertain?

A statement such as “AI makes mistakes” is a starting point. Show a specific mistake, explain why it matters, and test a possible improvement.

Final assignment: an AI project + post-mortem

Build an AI project around a research need that matters to you and demonstrate what it does. Accompany it with a post-mortem: an account of what worked, what failed, what you changed, and what you learned.

We will discuss your project ideas together. Bo will provide the detailed project brief later, including the submission requirements and deadline.

Session One: where the overview takes us

The overview retains three questions:

  1. Why do historians need computers?
  2. What can historians do with computers and AI?
  3. How can you work like a historian in the age of AI?

After the overview, we return to the connection between a research question, a workflow, and informed critique.

FAQ and tool check — about 20 minutes

  • 5 minutes: collect questions from your questions.md.
  • 8 minutes: check OpenCode, VS Code, your GitHub account, and your cloned fork.
  • 7 minutes: a volunteer demonstrates a task, and we examine any unclear steps or errors.

Keep unresolved questions for the course log. Bring the exact step and error when something does not work.

Live workshop

Bo will use a current model to investigate a question with the class. Everyone will inspect the output against the source, identify a blind spot, and try a change to the process. We will record the prompt, model, result, correction, and remaining uncertainty.

The live demonstration record will be added after class. The prompts and outcomes below are fields to complete, not a report of an experiment already performed.

Record To fill in during or after the workshop
Research question and source Question; source reference; page or image identifier
Model and tools Model name/version displayed; date; tool used
Prompt and task Exact prompt or link to the saved session
What happened Output or an excerpt linked to the original log
Check against the source Evidence supporting or contradicting the output
Blind spot and mitigation What failed; what changed; what happened on retest
Remaining questions Uncertainty and the next check to perform

Before sharing a log, remove passwords, API keys, and personal material that does not belong in the class record. Preserve the source and your checks so classmates can understand the result.

For next week: a small question and sources that need OCR

  1. Choose a small research question connected to your interests. For example: When was an institution founded, and what does the evidence actually establish? You do not need to use the same subject as the classroom demonstration.
  2. Find material in the library or an online library database.
  3. Use material that needs OCR: a scanned page, a photograph, or an image of text.
  4. Use OpenCode to investigate your question with that material.
  5. Check the OCR and the resulting claim against the original page.
  6. Bring your findings to the next class and explain how you assessed the material.

Show your research question, where the source came from, the original page beside its OCR text, how you used OpenCode, what you checked or corrected, and what remains uncertain. A result with clearly explained limits is useful work.

Next session: OCR

How do we get AI to read a source? We will move from a shared sample to your own material and examine how recognition errors affect research claims.
